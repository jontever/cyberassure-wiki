# Domain 2 — AI Risk Management

**Exam weight: 31%**

This domain confirms the ability to assess and manage the risks, threats, vulnerabilities, and supply chain issues that come with enterprise-wide AI adoption. It moves from the governance structures of Domain 1 into the practical discipline of identifying, measuring, and treating AI risk — including the distinctive threat landscape of AI systems and the accountability challenges of an AI supply chain.

## A. AI risk assessment, thresholds, and treatment

Measuring AI risk and deciding how to respond to it.

- **Impact assessment** — evaluating the potential consequences of an AI system on the organisation, individuals, and society.
- **Conformity assessment** — checking an AI system against applicable regulatory and standards requirements.
- **PIAs (Privacy Impact Assessments)** — assessing privacy risk where AI processes personal data.
- **Risk documentation** — recording identified risks, assessments, and decisions.
- **Acceptable levels of risk** — defining thresholds aligned to the enterprise risk appetite from Domain 1.
- **Treatment plans** — choosing to mitigate, transfer, accept, or avoid each risk.
- **KRIs and KPIs for AI use** — indicators that track AI risk exposure and control effectiveness over time.

## B. AI threat and vulnerability management

Identifying and managing the specific ways AI systems can be attacked or fail. (In ISACA's outline this sub-domain is titled to cover testing, threats, and vulnerabilities.)

- **Penetration testing** — adversarial testing of AI systems and their surrounding infrastructure.
- **Vulnerability tests** — identifying weaknesses in models, pipelines, and integrations.
- **Red teaming** — structured adversarial exercises against AI, including prompt-based and model-level attacks.
- **AI-related vulnerabilities** — model-specific weaknesses such as prompt injection, model inversion, membership inference, and evasion.
- **Adversarial threats** — deliberate manipulation of inputs or training data to cause incorrect behaviour.
- **Threat intelligence** — sourcing and applying intelligence on AI-specific threats.
- **AI-enabled threats / attack chains** — how adversaries use AI to enhance their own operations.
- **Anomalies** — detecting abnormal model behaviour or usage.
- **Threat landscape** — maintaining an up-to-date view of AI threats.
- **Deep fakes** — synthetic media used for fraud, impersonation, or disinformation.
- **Insider threat** — misuse of AI by trusted individuals.
- **AI agents** — the emerging risks of autonomous, tool-using agentic systems.

## C. AI vendor and supply chain management

Managing the risk introduced by third parties across the AI supply chain.

- **Dependencies of software packages and libraries** — the open-source and third-party components in AI stacks.
- **Vendor due diligence and contracts** — assessing and contractually binding AI vendors.
- **SLAs** — service-level commitments for AI-enabled services.
- **Vendor usage** — understanding how vendors actually use enterprise data and models.
- **Accountability models**
  - **Provider vs. deployer** — the differing responsibilities of those who build AI and those who deploy it (a key EU AI Act distinction).
  - **Third, fourth, and fifth parties** — risk cascading down the supply chain beyond direct vendors.
- **Ownership and intellectual property** — who owns models, outputs, and training data.
- **Access controls** — restricting vendor access to systems and data.
- **Liability** — allocating responsibility when AI causes harm.
- **Vendor monitoring for risk and changes** — ongoing oversight, not just point-in-time assessment.
