# Domain 1 — AI Governance and Program Management

**Exam weight: 31%**

This domain covers the ability to advise stakeholders on implementing AI security through effective policy, data governance, program management, and incident response. It is the governance backbone of the credential: establishing who is accountable for AI, what rules apply, how AI assets and data are managed through their life cycle, how the security program is run, and how AI incidents are handled.

## A. Stakeholder considerations, industry frameworks, and regulatory requirements

Establishing the governance context for AI before controls are chosen.

- **Organisational structure and overall governance** — where AI accountability sits and how it integrates with existing IT and security governance.
- **Roles and responsibilities** — clear ownership across business, security, data science, legal, and privacy.
- **Charter and steering committee** — a formal mandate and a cross-functional body to direct AI governance.
- **Identifying stakeholders** — internal (executives, developers, risk, legal) and external (regulators, customers, vendors).
- **Risk appetite and tolerance** — defining how much AI-related risk the enterprise will accept.
- **Frameworks, standards, and regulations** — e.g. NIST AI RMF, ISO/IEC 42001, ISO/IEC 23894, the EU AI Act, and sector-specific rules.
- **Selecting appropriate frameworks** — matching frameworks to the organisation's sector, jurisdiction, and maturity.
- **Business and use cases for AI** — grounding governance in the actual ways AI creates value and exposure.
- **Privacy considerations** — data protection obligations triggered by AI's use of personal data.

## B. AI-related strategies, policies, and procedures

Turning governance intent into documented direction.

- **AI strategy** — aligning AI adoption with business objectives and the security posture.
  - **Consumer vs. enterprise** — different risk profiles for public/consumer AI versus enterprise-controlled systems.
  - **Buy vs. build** — the security and accountability trade-offs of procuring vs. developing AI.
- **AI policies**
  - **Responsible use** — ethical, lawful, and fair use principles.
  - **Acceptable use** — what staff may and may not do with AI tools.
- **AI procedures**
  - **Implementation** — operational steps to deploy AI securely.
  - **Manuals** — documented working practice.
  - **Ethics** — embedding ethical guardrails into procedure.

## C. AI asset and data life cycle management

Knowing what AI assets and data exist and protecting them from creation to destruction.

- **AI asset and data inventory**
  - **Inventory management** — a maintained register of models, datasets, and AI services.
  - **Model cards** — standardised documentation of a model's purpose, training data, performance, and limitations.
- **Data handling, classification, discovery** — identifying and labelling data used by AI according to sensitivity.
- **Data augmentation and cleaning** — managing the integrity of training and input data.
- **Data storage** — secure, access-controlled storage for AI data.
- **Data protection** — encryption, masking, and access control across the life cycle.
- **Destruction** — secure disposal of data and models at end of life.

## D. AI security program development and management

Running AI security as a managed program, aligned to the wider security function.

- **Documented program plan** — a defined AI security program with objectives and scope.
- **Security team, roles, responsibilities, and proficiencies** — the skills and structure to deliver it.
- **Alignment to existing information security** — integrating AI security into the established ISMS rather than running it in a silo.
- **Use of AI-enabled security tools in the program** — leveraging AI to strengthen defence, with awareness of its own risks.
- **Metrics and management**
  - **KRIs and KPIs for AI use** with regard to security.
  - **Management reporting** — communicating AI security posture to leadership.

## E. Business continuity and incident response

Preparing for and responding to AI-specific incidents and disruptions.

- **Incident detection** — identifying AI-specific incidents (e.g. model compromise, harmful output).
- **Notification** — informing stakeholders and regulators as required.
- **Incident classification** — categorising by type and impact.
- **Criticality and severity** — prioritising response.
- **Resiliency** — designing AI systems and processes to withstand disruption.
- **Business continuity plan** — keeping essential functions running when AI is degraded or unavailable.
  - **Red-button requirements for compliance** — the ability to halt an AI system to meet regulatory obligations.
- **Incident response playbooks specifically for AI** — tailored runbooks for AI failure modes.
- **Break-glass policies / go–no-go** — emergency controls and decision gates.
- **Authority** — who is empowered to invoke emergency measures.
- **RTO / RPO — AI perspective** — recovery objectives adapted to AI systems and data.
- **Disaster recovery** — restoring AI capability after a major event.
- **Testing** — exercising the plans to confirm they work.
